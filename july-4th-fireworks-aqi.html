Fireworks vs. Air Quality: July 4th 2026 in time-lapse

By Danny Page

Every July 4th, millions of fireworks push a wave of particulate pollution into the air over a few hours. PurpleAir's sensor network makes that wave visible: thousands of low-cost sensors reporting AQI in near real time. I pointed a headless browser at the map, screenshotted six locations every two minutes overnight, and stitched the frames into time-lapses. The dots go green to yellow to orange to red as the smoke settles, then drain back to green by morning.

The time-lapses

Each clip runs from roughly 8 PM ET on the 4th to 9:30 AM ET on the 5th. The shape is the same everywhere: clean air, a sharp spike once the shows start, then a recovery whose speed comes down almost entirely to local wind. Where the air sits still, the smoke hangs around for hours.

How it's done

The whole thing is a small Python and shell pipeline driven by cron on a Mac. There is no PurpleAir API key involved. It drives a real headless browser against the public map and screenshots it, which sidesteps rate limits and captures exactly what a person would see.

  • scraper.sh is the cron entry point. It activates the virtualenv and runs the capture script.
  • capture.py reads the location list and, for each one, opens the PurpleAir map in headless Chromium at 1280×800, waits for real sensor data, strips the cookie banner and "get a sensor" promo, and saves a PNG into images/<year>/<name>/.
  • locations.conf lists the capture targets as name|zoom/lat/lon. Those become the URL hash on map.purpleair.com.
  • compile.sh stitches each location's frames into an mp4 with ffmpeg, in chronological order (the filenames embed a sortable timestamp).
  • crontab.txt is the schedule: capture every two minutes across the holiday window, then one compile pass the next afternoon.

The part that actually matters: waiting for the data

The naive version of this, "load the page, wait five seconds, screenshot," produces garbage. Half the frames come out with no dots because the sensor fetch hadn't finished, or because headless Chromium had throttled the "backgrounded" tab and delayed everything. Two fixes make the capture reliable.

First, wait for the actual network response instead of a fixed timer. PurpleAir loads its dots from a /v1/sensors request, so the script blocks on that response before continuing, then adds a short fixed settle for the map to paint the dots to the canvas.

with page.expect_response(is_sensors_response,
                          timeout=SENSORS_TIMEOUT_MS):
    page.goto(url)
page.wait_for_timeout(PAINT_SETTLE_MS)   # let mapbox paint the dots

Second, disable the headless throttling that otherwise stalls that fetch unpredictably:

ANTI_THROTTLE = [
    "--disable-background-timer-throttling",
    "--disable-renderer-backgrounding",
    "--disable-backgrounding-occluded-windows",
]

Data arrival is the variable part, so it gets an explicit wait. The paint step is CPU-bound and consistent, so a fixed delay is fine there. If the sensor response never shows up within the timeout, the script falls back to a plain settle and screenshots anyway, so every run still produces a frame.

At a two-minute cadence, one real hour becomes 30 frames. Compiled at 10 fps, that hour plays back in three seconds, which is why a full overnight window fits into a clip you can watch in a minute.

Bonus: 2024 Bay Area

I ran an earlier version of this in 2024 for the Bay Area. That year the smoke hung around far longer than 2026 did, a useful contrast to the quick clearout above.
